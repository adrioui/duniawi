# Orwell Writing

Apply Orwell's discipline from "Politics and the English Language". The goal is scrupulous, clear, honest English: say what you mean, in the fewest and simplest words that do the job, without dying metaphors, pretentious diction, or needless passives. It applies to fiction and non-fiction alike, but it is at its most valuable for prose that must inform, persuade, or endure.

## The six questions

A scrupulous writer asks at least four questions of every sentence, and probably two more:

1. **What am I trying to say?** — State the intended meaning before polishing the words.
2. **What words will express it?** — Choose words that carry that meaning, not words that merely look good.
3. **What image or idiom will make it clearer?** — Prefer a concrete image over an abstract claim when one is available.
4. **Is this image fresh enough to have an effect?** — Reject images that have gone stale through print.
5. **Could I put it more shortly?** — Cut anything that can be cut; shorter is usually clearer.
6. **Have I said anything that is avoidably ugly?** — Remove jarring, clumsy, or offensive phrasing you can simply avoid.

## The six rules

When instinct fails, rely on these rules, which cover most cases:

1. **Never use a metaphor, simile, or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print.** — Dying metaphors ("no axe to grind", "toe the line", "ride roughshod over") are used because they save people the trouble of inventing their own image. Fresh writing needs fresh images.
2. **Never use a long word where a short one will do.** — Prefer "use" over "utilize", "try" over "endeavor", "about" over "approximately". Long words are not more correct; they are usually vaguer.
3. **If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.** — Remove "very", "quite", "rather", "in order to", "in the case of", and every other word that contributes nothing.
4. **Never use the passive where you can use the active.** — "The committee decided" beats "a decision was made by the committee". The passive obscures agency; it is only justified when the agent is genuinely unknown or unimportant.
5. **Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word, or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent.** — Say "standard" instead of "de facto", "talk" instead of "dialogue". Jargon is acceptable only to the audience that needs it and cannot say it more plainly.
6. **Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.** — The rules serve honesty and clarity; they are not idols. Never write something ugly, false, or inhumane just to keep the rules.

## How to apply

- **Before writing**: for each planned sentence, know the answer to question 1. If you cannot say what you mean, you are not ready to write it.
- **While revising**: pass the text through the six questions sentence by sentence; then enforce the six rules as a checklist, longest to shortest.
- **When reviewing others' or AI-generated prose**: use the questions to diagnose and the rules to prescribe exact rewrites. Point to the specific rule each rewrite satisfies.
- **Balance**: do not strip voice to the bone. Orwell's rules are instruments of clarity and honesty, not a prohibition on style. When a phrase breaks a rule but is vivid, accurate, and necessary, that is rule 6 in action — but the exception must be deliberate, not lazy.
- **Know the source**: these come from Orwell's essay *"Politics and the English Language"* (1946), where he argues that sloppy prose and sloppy thinking feed each other — bad writing obscures politics, and clear writing is a defense of truth.

## Checklist for a final pass

- [ ] Every sentence answers "what am I trying to say?"
- [ ] No word is long when a short one does the same job.
- [ ] No word could be cut without losing meaning.
- [ ] Passives are gone where the active voice is possible.
- [ ] No foreign, scientific, or jargon term survives where an everyday word works.
- [ ] Any broken rule is a deliberate choice, not a default.
